Section =============================================================================== Evaluating frontier models on scientific tasks is challenging because success depends on both a model's domain knowledge and its reasoning capabilities. Existing benchmarks often don't distinguish between these two factors, making it difficult to determine if a model fails due to a lack of specialized knowledge or an inability to apply that knowledge logically. Existing approaches like the ChemBench and MaCBench[#1] datasets try to address this challenge, but they have limitations. ChemBench manually tags tasks as requiring reasoning, but this is a binary, heuristic-based judgment that doesn't capture the varying degrees of reasoning complexity. Similarly, MaCBench's ablation studies, which simplify scientific terminology, are a good step, but even "plain knowledge" versions of tasks can still implicitly require some level of domain-specific understanding. The core issue remains: it's difficult to quantify the exact contribution of knowledge versus reasoning to a model's performance. This is further complicated by the fact that the complexity of both the required reasoning and the domain-specific knowledge can vary significantly within a single task. A single problem might demand simple factual recall in one part and complex, multi-step deduction in another, making a single "reasoning required" tag inadequate for a comprehensive evaluation. This makes it hard to pinpoint whether a model's failure is due to a simple knowledge gap or a more fundamental inability to reason. In an ideal benchmarking scenario, we would have benchmarks that can isolate these factors and provide a clearer picture of a model's strengths and weaknesses.
